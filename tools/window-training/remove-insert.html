Path B

Changing Out an Old Insert

The original sashes went years ago and somebody put a vinyl or aluminum unit in their place. That unit has to come all the way out before you measure anything.

The mistake this whole page exists to prevent

The old insert has a frame of its own, and that frame is inside the original wood one. Measure while it is still there and you are measuring the old vinyl, not the opening. The window comes back an inch or more too small, and there is nothing to do with it but eat it.

Why the opening gets bigger

Two frames, one opening. Pull the inner one and you get its wall thickness back on every side — typically ¾″ to 1¼″ on the width, and the same again on the height.

The vinyl is new. The wood behind it is not.

A 1990s insert in a 1920s house means 1920s paint on the frame you are about to scrape. If the house predates 1978, this is a lead job the moment you disturb that wood — the shiny window out front changes nothing. Eye protection and cut gloves throughout; cutting old vinyl throws chips.

Work out what you are looking at

Before anything comes off, figure out how the old unit was fitted. Look for:

  • A vinyl or aluminum frame sitting inside the wood one — a visible second frame all the way round
  • A caulk joint where that frame meets the original wood
  • Screw heads in the side jambs, usually hidden behind the balance covers or under a snap-in trim strip
  • Expander trim at the head, and a sloped adapter at the sill
  • Whether the original inside stops are still there, or whether the old install ripped them off
Photograph it before you touch it

Especially the corners and the sill. When you are trying to remember how the trim went together three hours later, the photo is worth more than your memory.

Strip the old unit out

Sashes out first — they come out the same way they do on any modern insert, by tilting or by releasing the balance clips. A stripped frame is far easier to handle and nothing is going to break and cut you.

If it still will not come out, cut it — carefully

Old units were often foamed in solid. Cut through the vinyl frame at the corners with an oscillating tool or a fine-tooth blade and collapse it inward, so the pressure goes away from the wood. Set the blade depth to the vinyl only. A blade into the original jamb turns a routine change-out into a frame repair.

Get back to bare, sound wood

What is left is somebody else's install, and you are about to warranty whatever you leave behind it.

  • Cut and pull every bit of old foam out of the perimeter — it hides both rot and screws
  • Scrape the old caulk off the jamb faces so the new unit sits flat
  • Pull every leftover screw, shim and nail
  • Check whether the old install removed the parting bead and blind stop, or just buried them — anything left in the pocket changes your depth
  • Probe the sill and jamb bottoms with an awl. Old inserts leak at the sill, and that is exactly where you will find the damage
  • Look for water staining on the interior casing — it tells you the last one failed and why
  • Fill, sand, and prime bare wood before you measure
Expect to find rot here more often than on Path A

An insert that has been leaking quietly for twenty years has been feeding water straight into the original sill. If the sill is soft, no amount of foam and caulk on the new unit will fix it. Repair the wood or price the opening as a full-frame — and tell the customer before you order, not after.

Now — and only now — measure

Old unit gone, foam out, caulk scraped, wood sound and primed. The opening in front of you is a different size than the one you started the morning with, and this is the one that counts.
