How to Measure

You measure weeks before you install, on a window that still works. Three widths, three heights, what is hidden behind the stops, both diagonals and the depth. Smallest number wins, then take off a half inch.

Measure the window exactly as you find it

Nothing comes off. No stops, no bead, no sashes. You take these numbers on an intact, working window, the order goes in, and the window shows up. Install day is the only day anything comes apart — and by then the replacement is already sitting in the van.

Every reading here is taken between the inside stops, where the sashes run. That is not the whole opening — the stops are covering the last bit of it on each side. Section 2 is how you get that bit back.

1. Width — three readings between the inside stops

Lay the tape across the window and read the clear distance between the two inside stops at the top, the middle, and the bottom. Old jambs bow. They are almost never the same three times.

2. The add-back — what the stops are hiding

The inside stop sits against the jamb and covers the last bit of the opening on each side. Your tape stops at the face of the stop; the jamb carries on behind it. To find out how much, slide something blade-thin in beside the stop until it stops dead on the jamb.

What to use for a blade

Paint stores sell a rule for reading paint depth in a cup — about the thickness of a feeler gauge, marked in sixteenths, and it is the right tool for this. A machinist's rule or a wet-film gauge does the same job. What matters is that it is thin enough to go in without forcing anything and stiff enough to feel the jamb when it lands.

On Path B — where an old insert is already in the frame — that joint is nearly always caulked. Push the blade straight through the caulk. You are looking for the original wood jamb behind it, and on those openings the hidden amount is bigger, because the old unit's frame is in there too.

3. Height — three readings, the same way

Drop the tape from under the head stop to the sill at the left, the centre, and the right. Because the sill slopes, always come down to its highest point — the back edge, nearest the room. Then add back at the head and at the sill, exactly as you did on the width.

Why the high point matters

4. Square — measure both diagonals

Corner to corner, both ways, between the stops like everything else. You are after the difference between the two numbers, not the numbers themselves — so the stops hiding a bit at each corner does not matter here. If the two match, the opening is square and the window will drop in and operate. If they do not, you already know you will be shimming and scribing — and if it is bad enough, you order tighter to buy yourself room.

Diagonal differenceWhat it meansWhat you do
Under ¼″Square enough.Order normally. Standard shimming.
¼″ to ½″Noticeably out.Order normally but expect to shim hard on one side and scribe the stops.
Over ½″Badly racked.Flag it. Consider ordering an extra ¼″ down, and warn the customer the trim will need scribing.

5. Depth — will an insert even fit?

Pocket depth runs from the face of the blind stop to the face of the inside stop, and you get it without pulling the parting bead. Slide the lower sash up a few inches and take the reading below it, where both sash channels are clear. Butt the tape's hook against the blind stop and pull back toward the inside stop — the hook stands the blade up off the jamb, so it rides straight over the bead.

A standard insert wants about 3¼″. Check the spec sheet for the unit you actually sell — some run as little as 2¾″.

If the pocket will not make depth, stop. That is a full-frame replacement, and it needs to be priced as one before anyone talks to the customer about money. There is a drawn-out section on this in Anatomy & Terms.

6. The deduction — half an inch, every time

Take ½″ off the width and ½″ off the height, off the tight opening in each direction — the smallest reading with the add-backs already in it. That gap is what lets you shim the unit square in an opening that is not, and it is what you pack with insulation afterwards. It is not slop — it is the working room the install depends on.

Round down, never up

If your arithmetic lands between eighths, go to the smaller eighth. A window a sixteenth too small gets shimmed. A window a sixteenth too big goes back on the truck.

7. Write it down before you leave the room

Six readings, four add-backs, two diagonals and one depth, per opening — and every opening in the house is its own set. Nobody remembers that walking down a ladder, and nobody is coming back to check before the order goes in.

  • Width between the stops at top, middle, bottom
  • Height between the stops at left, centre, right
  • Hidden behind the left stop, and behind the right stop
  • Hidden at the head, and at the sill
  • Both diagonals
  • Pocket depth
  • A name for the opening you will still understand next week — “kitchen, over the sink”, not “window 4”
  • Anything odd: rot, a bowed jamb, a sill that is shot, paint you suspect is lead
